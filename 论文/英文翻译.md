(Zn1−xMgx)2TiO4\mathrm{(Zn_{1-x}Mg_x)_2TiO_4}(Zn1−x​Mgx​)2​TiO4​ spinel phase.With increasing Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content, the ZnO diffraction peaks first shift to higher angles and then Zinc oxide (ZnO)-based ceramic linear resistors offer abundant raw materials, mature processing, high energy absorption capability, and widely adjustable resistivity.Therefore, they are widely used in power-system neutral grounding, current-limiting voltage dividers, and high-power electronic circuits [1,2].Unlike ZnO varistors that rely on a high nonlinear coefficient (α\alphaα) for overvoltage protection [3,4], ZnO linear resistors must follow Ohm's law (α\alphaα close to 1.00) with stable resistivity and a low temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) [5,6].However, ZnO is a non-stoichiometric nnn-type wide-bandgap semiconductor, and the electrical transport of polycrystalline ZnO ceramics is controlled by both intragranular point defects and grain boundary barriers [7,8].Acceptor states at grain boundaries easily trap free electrons from ZnO grains, creating a negatively charged layer, a depletion layer, and double Schottky barriers that impede electron transport [9–11].When the grain boundary barrier is high, the grain boundary resistance drops with increasing electric field, resulting in non-ohmic current–voltage (I–VI\text{–}VI–V) characteristics [12,13].Therefore, proper control of both grain conductivity and the grain boundary barrier is the key to achieving stable ohmic characteristics [5,6].
Previous studies show that the ZnO–MgO–TiO2\mathrm{ZnO\text{–}MgO\text{–}TiO_2}ZnO–MgO–TiO2​ (ZMT) system is a good base composition for ZnO linear resistors [5,6].MgO has a wide bandgap (Eg≈7.8E_\mathrm{g} \approx 7.8Eg​≈7.8 eV), and Mg2+\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}Mg2+ substitution at Zn2+\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}Zn2+ sites modifies the local electronic structure and bandgap of ZnO [14].This doping also regulates carrier transport, increases resistivity, and improves resistance–temperature stability [15].Meanwhile, TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ has limited solubility in ZnO, so excess TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ reacts with ZnO and MgO during sintering to form a (Zn1−xMgx)2TiO4\mathrm{(Zn_{1-x}Mg_x)_2TiO_4}(Zn1−x​Mgx​)2​TiO4​ spinel phase near grain boundaries. This spinel phase inhibits excessive ZnO grain growth and improves ceramic density [16,17].This spinel phase inhibits excessive ZnO grain growth and improves ceramic density [16,17].In our previous research, we carried out a series of studies on ZMT and related ZnO-based ceramics.In Al-doped systems, reducing atmosphere sintering [18,19] and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [20] greatly increased donor solubility and electron-active defect concentrations, yielding highly conductive ZnO ceramics.In In- or Ga-doped ZMT ceramics, atmosphere control effectively lowered the grain boundary barrier, achieving high-conductivity near-ohmic behavior [21] and low resistivity (0.10–0.41 Ω·cm) with good linearity (α=1.01–1.02\alpha = 1.01\text{–}1.02α=1.01–1.02) [6].Moreover, adding La2O3\mathrm{La_2O_3}La2​O3​ to air-sintered ZMT ceramics caused a transition from non-ohmic to ohmic characteristics (α=1.00\alpha = 1.00α=1.00), but the resistivity also decreased to 3.58–13.18 Ω·cm [5].
The above studies mainly increased donor defect concentrations or lowered grain boundary barriers to achieve high conductivity and good linearity, which suits low-resistivity ZnO ceramics.However, practical applications often require linear resistors to cover a wider resistivity range.Many studies have tailored the microstructure and electrical properties of ZnO-based linear resistance ceramics by adding insulating secondary phases (such as ZnAl2O4\mathrm{ZnAl_2O_4}ZnAl2​O4​ [22] or high-content Al2O3\mathrm{Al_2O_3}Al2​O3​ [23]) or doping with metal oxides including Sm2O3\mathrm{Sm_2O_3}Sm2​O3​ [2], MgO [15], TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ [16], Fe2O3\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}Fe2​O3​ [24], NiO [25], CaO [26], La2O3\mathrm{La_2O_3}La2​O3​ [27], ZrO2\mathrm{ZrO_2}ZrO2​ [28], and Sc2O3\mathrm{Sc_2O_3}Sc2​O3​ [29].However, existing systems still struggle to combine higher resistivity, excellent I–VI\text{–}VI–V linearity, and a low TCR.Some studies on insulating-phase composites focused mainly on resistivity and resistance–temperature behavior, without fully testing DC linearity under operating currents [22,23].In most multi-oxide doped systems, higher-resistivity samples fail to maintain ideal linear I–VI\text{–}VI–V curves (for example, the optimal α\alphaα remains 1.05–1.20 for TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ [16], Fe2O3\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}Fe2​O3​ [24], CaO [26], and La2O3\mathrm{La_2O_3}La2​O3​ [27] doping), or they exhibit a large negative TCR [16].Even in the same ZMT system, high-resistivity undoped samples show obvious non-ohmic behavior (α=1.98\alpha = 1.98α=1.98) [5].Therefore, raising the resistivity of ZMT ceramics while maintaining near-ohmic behavior and a low TCR still needs further study.
Based on these considerations, Li was chosen as an acceptor dopant to adjust the resistivity of ZMT ceramics.As a group-IA element, four-coordinated Li+\mathrm{Li^+}Li+ has an ionic radius (0.59 Å) close to that of Zn2+\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}Zn2+ (0.60 Å) [30].At low doping levels, Li+\mathrm{Li^+}Li+ easily replaces Zn2+\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}Zn2+ in the ZnO lattice to form LiZn′\mathrm{Li_{Zn}'}LiZn′​ acceptors, which compensate free electrons to lower carrier concentration and raise grain resistivity [31,32] without markedly increasing the grain boundary barrier.However, theoretical calculations [32–35] and spectroscopic studies [36] show that Li exhibits amphoteric behavior in ZnO.As the Li content increases, the formation energy of interstitial donors (Lii∙\mathrm{Li_i^\bullet}Lii∙​) decreases, so some Li+\mathrm{Li^+}Li+ ions enter interstitial sites and combine with LiZn′\mathrm{Li_{Zn}'}LiZn′​ to form neutral (LiZn′–Lii∙)×(\mathrm{Li_{Zn}'\text{–}Li_i^\bullet)^\times}(LiZn′​–Lii∙​)× self-compensating defect pairs.Meanwhile, excess Li tends to segregate at grain boundaries, changing the grain boundary resistance and barrier height [11,37].In this work, trace Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ was added to air-sintered ZnO–MgO–TiO2\mathrm{ZnO\text{–}MgO\text{–}TiO_2}ZnO–MgO–TiO2​ (ZMT) ceramics to study the effects of Li content on microstructure, defect evolution, and electrical properties (resistivity, I–VI\text{–}VI–V linearity, and temperature stability).This work aims to raise the resistivity while achieving good near-ohmic characteristics and a low TCR.

ZMT-based ceramics were prepared by the solid-state reaction method with a molar composition of (96.5−x)(96.5 - x)(96.5−x) mol% ZnO + 3 mol% MgO + 0.5 mol% TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ + xxx mol% Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ (x=0,0.010,0.014,0.018,0.022,x = 0, 0.010, 0.014, 0.018, 0.022,x=0,0.010,0.014,0.018,0.022, and 0.0260.0260.026).
Because the Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content is very low, the weighed Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ was first fully dissolved in deionized water to ensure uniform Li distribution, and then wet ball-milled with the ZnO, MgO, and TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ powders.
The powders were mixed with 1.1 times their weight of deionized water and 4 times their weight of zirconia balls, and milled for 6 h to obtain a uniform slurry.
The slurry was dried at 120 °C for 4 h, and the dried powder was ground, sieved, and calcined at 570 °C for 3 h.
Next, 1 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) binder was added for granulation, and the powder was pressed at 150 MPa for 15–30 s into pellets about 12 mm in diameter and 1.8 mm in thickness.
The green pellets were heated at 550 °C for 3 h to remove the binder, then sintered in air at 1200 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C/min, and finally furnace-cooled.
The sintered samples were ground and polished on both sides to a thickness of about 1.0 mm, followed by ultrasonic cleaning and drying.
For electrical measurements, silver paste was uniformly coated on both surfaces and fired to form silver electrodes with good ohmic contact.
Based on the Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content, the six sample groups were labeled ZMT, ZMT-L10, ZMT-L14, ZMT-L18, ZMT-L22, and ZMT-L26.
Here, ZMT is the undoped matrix sample, L denotes Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ addition, and 10, 14, 18, 22, and 26 correspond to 0.010, 0.014, 0.018, 0.022, and 0.026 mol% Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​, respectively.

X-ray diffraction (XRD, D/max 2550V, Rigaku, Japan) was used to analyze the phase composition and crystal structure of the samples.The measurements used Cu Kα\alphaα radiation at 40 kV and 10 mA over a 2θ2\theta2θ range of 20°–80° with a step size of 0.05°.Phase analysis was performed using Jade software, and shifts of the main ZnO diffraction peaks were examined to study the effect of Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ on the ZnO lattice.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HPZ440/Aspex, Phenom, Netherlands) was operated at 15 kV in backscattered electron (BSE) mode to observe the microstructure.
The sample surfaces were ground, polished, ultrasonically cleaned, and thermally etched at 1100 °C for 10 min to reveal grain boundaries and determine the average ZnO grain size.
Room-temperature I–VI\text{–}VI–V curves were measured using a digital source meter (Keithley 2450, USA) under a DC current sweep of 0–1 A.
Using the effective electrode area SSS and sample thickness LLL, the I–VI\text{–}VI–V curves were converted into J–EJ\text{–}EJ–E curves (J=I/SJ = I/SJ=I/S is current density and E=V/LE = V/LE=V/L is electric field intensity) to calculate the DC resistivity ρ\rhoρ and nonlinear coefficient α\alphaα.
Room-temperature complex impedance spectra were measured by broadband dielectric spectroscopy (Concept 40, Novocontrol, Germany) from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz with an AC voltage of 1 V, and fitted with equivalent circuit models using Z-View software.
The effective Hall carrier concentration (nHn_\mathrm{H}nH​) and Hall mobility (μH\mu_\mathrm{H}μH​) were measured at room temperature by the van der Pauw method using a Hall effect system (HMS-5500, ECOPIA, South Korea) at 0.545 T and 20 mA.
To evaluate temperature stability, the samples were heated from room temperature to 150 °C at 2.5 °C/min in a temperature-controlled chamber, and the resistance was recorded with a digital multimeter using a two-probe setup.
Raman scattering spectra were recorded from 80 to 1250 cm−1\mathrm{cm^{-1}}cm−1 using a confocal micro-Raman spectrometer (inVia, Renishaw, UK) with a 532 nm laser, and peak fitting was performed after baseline subtraction.
UV–vis absorption spectra were measured from 200 to 800 nm using a spectrophotometer (U-4100, Hitachi, Japan), and the optical bandgap EgE_\mathrm{g}Eg​ was determined by extrapolating the linear region of the Tauc plot for a direct-bandgap semiconductor.

Fig. 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of ZMT ceramics with different Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ contents along with the standard hexagonal wurtzite ZnO card (JCPDS No. 36-1451).As shown in Fig. 1(a), the main diffraction peaks of all samples match well with hexagonal wurtzite ZnO [5,6].A weak secondary peak also appears near 2θ=35.3∘2\theta = 35.3^\circ2θ=35.3∘, corresponding to the (311) plane of cubic spinel Zn2TiO4\mathrm{Zn_2TiO_4}Zn2​TiO4​ (JCPDS No. 18-1487).This secondary phase forms through the high-temperature reaction between ZnO and excess TiO2\mathrm{TiO_2}TiO2​ beyond its solubility limit during sintering.Meanwhile, part of Zn2+\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}Zn2+ in the Zn2TiO4\mathrm{Zn_2TiO_4}Zn2​TiO4​ lattice is substituted by Mg2+\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}Mg2+, forming a (Zn1−xMgx)2TiO4\mathrm{(Zn_{1-x}Mg_x)_2TiO_4}(Zn1−x​Mgx​)2​TiO4​ solid-solution spinel phase [5,17].Moreover, no Li-containing or other impurity peaks are detected with increasing Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content, showing that trace Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ addition does not change the basic phase composition of ZMT ceramics.
In the enlarged XRD patterns from 2θ=34∘2\theta = 34^\circ2θ=34∘ to 37∘37^\circ37∘ (Fig. 1(b)), the main ZnO peaks gradually shift to higher angles as the Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content increases from 0 to 0.018 mol%, reaching a maximum shift in ZMT-L18.When the Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content increases further to 0.022 and 0.026 mol%, the peaks shift back to lower angles.According to Bragg's law (2dsin⁡θ=nλ2d\sin\theta = n\lambda2dsinθ=nλ), a shift to higher angles means a smaller interplanar spacing ddd, whereas a shift to lower angles means a larger d.Thus, the ZnO lattice first contracts and then expands with increasing Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​ content [38].At 0–0.018 mol%, part of four-coordinated Zn2+\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}Zn2+ (0.60 Å) is replaced by slightly smaller four-coordinated Li+\mathrm{Li^+}Li+ (0.59 Å) [30].Meanwhile, LiZn′\mathrm{Li_{Zn}'}LiZn′​ acceptors alter local charge balance and distort the local Zn–O coordination [32,36], so both effects contract the unit cell and shift the ZnO peaks to higher angles.At 0.022 and 0.026 mol% Li2CO3\mathrm{Li_2CO_3}Li2​CO3​, the fraction of substitutional Li decreases, and part of Li+\mathrm{Li^+}Li+ enters interstitial sites to form interstitial donors (Lii∙\mathrm{Li_i^\bullet}Lii∙​) and self-compensating defect pairs [32,34].The local lattice expansion from interstitial Li weakens the lattice contraction seen at lower doping levels [32,34,38].In addition, grain growth at high doping levels (Fig. 2) reduces the grain boundary area and helps release local strain, shifting the main ZnO peaks of ZMT-L22 and ZMT-L26 back to lower angles.
SEM in BSE mode was used to further study the microstructure and phase distribution of the samples.Fig. 2(a–f) shows the thermally etched BSE-SEM images of ZMT, ZMT-L10, ZMT-L14, ZMT-L18, ZMT-L22, and ZMT-L26, with the grain size distributions in the insets.In Fig. 2, all samples are densely sintered, with ZnO as the main grains and clear grain boundaries.The small black dots near the grain boundaries are the (Zn1−xMgx)2TiO4\mathrm{(Zn_{1-x}Mg_x)_2TiO_4}(Zn1−x​Mgx​)2​TiO4​ spinel phase [5,6], which agrees with the XRD results.In addition, a few round dark spots are residual pores or pull-out pits formed during polishing and thermal etching.
